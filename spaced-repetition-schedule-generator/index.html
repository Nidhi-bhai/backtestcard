How the interval schedule works

Spaced repetition rests on the forgetting curve: memory of new material drops fast in the first days, and each successful recall slows the next drop. Reviewing at growing gaps (for example 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days) gives you more retention per hour than re-reading the same night. The exact numbers are a rule of thumb, not a law. Apps like Anki adjust intervals per card based on how hard recall felt. A fixed schedule like this one is simpler and works well for chapters, lectures and topics rather than individual flashcards.

Tips that make it work

  • Review by recalling first (close the book, write or say what you remember), then check. Re-reading alone is much weaker.
  • If a review goes badly, restart that topic from the first interval.
  • Keep each review short: 10–20 minutes per topic is usually enough.
  • Don't let reviews pile up. If one day has too many, spread the overflow by a day; small delays matter less than skipping.

FAQ

What is a good spaced repetition schedule?

A common starting point is to review 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days after first learning something. Shorten the gaps for hard material or a close exam; lengthen them for easy material.

How often should you do spaced repetition?

Daily, in short sessions. Each day you only review the topics that are due, which this generator lists by date.

Can I use this with Google Calendar?

Yes. Download the .ics file and import it in Google Calendar (Settings, Import & export). Each review appears as an all-day event.

Is spaced repetition better than re-reading?

Research on the spacing effect and retrieval practice consistently finds that spaced, active recall beats massed re-reading for long-term retention.
